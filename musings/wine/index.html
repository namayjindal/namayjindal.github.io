musings, no. 1

wine, from the top

i hosted a wine night and realised, about an hour before, that i couldn't explain a single thing on the label.

september 2026 · six minutes · drawn in python

what i wanted wasn't tasting vocabulary. i didn't need to say "notes of graphite" at anybody. i wanted the shape of the thing — why any of this varies at all, and what the words on a bottle are actually pointing at.

it turns out most of it collapses into two ideas. once you have them, the rest stops being trivia you have to memorise and starts being stuff you can just work out.

good wine starts with a plant having a bad time

the word you'll hear is terroir, which sounds precious, but it's really just a list: climate, soil, slope, water, which grape you planted, how old the vine is, and what the winemaker decides to do about all of it.

the part that genuinely surprised me is the soil. you'd assume rich soil gives better fruit. it's the opposite. rich, wet, comfortable soil produces a vine that spends its energy on leaves and volume — plenty of grapes, all of them watery. poor rocky soil that drains fast produces a vine that has to send roots down a long way, and can only support a few clusters. those clusters are the concentrated ones.

a comfortable vine makes leaves. a struggling one makes wine. i have thought about this more than i expected to.

water works the same way — enough to live, a little stress at the right moment, and the fruit improves. so does yield: a vine pushed to produce as much as physically possible produces thin wine. so does vine age, since older vines naturally give you less.

terroir is just: a specific place, plus a person making decisions about it. that's why the label tells you where.

red and white are not different fruits

this is the one that reorganised everything else for me.

cut open almost any wine grape — dark-skinned or pale — and the flesh inside is clear. the juice is essentially colourless either way. all of the colour, and most of the tannin, lives in the skin.

so the colour of a wine isn't really a property of the grape at all. it's a property of how long the juice sat with the skins.

which explains three things at once:

  • red — dark grapes, skins left in through fermentation.
  • white — pale grapes; or dark grapes pressed off fast enough that no colour ever transfers.
  • orange — white grapes treated exactly like a red. skins left in. that's the whole trick.
orange wine isn't a grape, or a blend, or a marketing thing. it's a white grape given the red treatment. the name does it a disservice.

it's also why rosé exists in the middle, and why nobody has to grow a special pink grape to make it. you just stop early.

champagne is a process, not a colour

sparkling sits on a completely separate axis from red / white / rosé. it's about whether there are bubbles, which is a manufacturing decision rather than anything to do with the fruit.

champagne can only come from champagne, the region in france. the same method made anywhere else gets a different name — crémant elsewhere in france, cava in spain, prosecco in italy, plain "sparkling wine" in the states.

it's made mostly from chardonnay (pale) plus pinot noir and pinot meunier (both dark). two of the three grapes are dark, and it still comes out pale — because the juice is pressed off the skins immediately. which you already know how to reason about now.

the bubbles themselves: you make a normal still wine first. then you add a bit more yeast and sugar, seal it into the bottle, and let it ferment a second time in there. fermentation produces CO2. the CO2 has nowhere to escape to, so it dissolves into the wine instead.

afterwards it sits on the dead yeast — the lees — for months or years, and that's where the bready, toasty, brioche thing comes from. it isn't a flavouring anyone adds. it's just time spent with yeast.

prosecco does its second fermentation in a big tank and then bottles it. faster, cheaper, less complex. champagne does it inside the exact bottle you get handed. you're holding the pressure vessel.

the four reds, placed

the useful thing here isn't tasting notes, it's a number line. body and tannin, light to heavy. everything else is detail hung off this.

  • pinot noir — the lightest. high acid, low tannin, red fruit like cherry and raspberry, plus something earthy, almost mushroomy in the french ones. thin-skinned and genuinely difficult to grow, which is most of why it costs what it costs.
  • merlot — medium bodied, soft, low tannin, plum and black cherry. the bottle to hand somebody who says they don't like red, because what they usually mean is they don't like tannin.
  • sangiovese — what chianti is made of. same weight as merlot but noticeably more acid: tart, savoury, clearly built to be drunk with food rather than alone.
  • cabernet sauvignon — heaviest and most tannic. tannin is that drying, grippy feeling across your gums. it's a texture, not a taste, which took me embarrassingly long to separate.

merlot and cabernet get blended together in bordeaux for exactly the reason you'd guess from the line: the cabernet brings structure and grip, the merlot softens it back down.

the entire decision tree at the shop: liked the chianti, want lighter → pinot noir. liked it, want softer → merlot. want more of everything → cabernet.

what i'd do differently

buy three bottles along the line instead of three at random, and pour them in that order. side by side the differences are obvious. one at a time, on separate evenings, they're basically invisible.

that's the whole lesson, and it cost me one mediocre wine night to find out.
